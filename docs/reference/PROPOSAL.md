# Proposal to Revenue Services Lesotho

## Adaptive Tax Intelligence and Investigation System

**Prepared by Vati AI**
**RSL Taxathon — Challenge 2**
**November 2026**

---

# Executive Summary

Revenue Services Lesotho faces an increasingly complex tax-compliance environment. Multinational structures, related-party transactions, persistent VAT refund positions, imported services, expatriate arrangements, foreign payments and rapidly changing financial technologies create tax risks that cannot always be understood by examining a single return, invoice or taxpayer in isolation.

At the same time, the objective of tax administration cannot simply be to identify more taxpayers as suspicious. Legitimate commercial arrangements must be recognised, genuine VAT refunds must be processed promptly, compliant taxpayers must not be subjected to unnecessary investigations, and every action taken by RSL must remain supported by law and defensible evidence.

Challenge 2 reflects this balance. It identifies profit shifting through related-party transactions, in-house loss creation, persistent VAT credit positions, imported services and uncertainty between employment and consultancy arrangements as key areas requiring stronger investigation capability. It also makes clear that risk is not the same as wrongdoing and that legitimate refunds must continue to be processed efficiently.

Vati AI proposes an **Adaptive Tax Intelligence and Investigation System** that strengthens the full process from identifying risk to resolving a case.

The system does not seek to replace RSL's existing tax systems, e-invoicing infrastructure, audit processes or officers. Instead, it provides an intelligence and investigation layer that connects information already available to RSL, identifies what requires attention, assembles relevant evidence, helps officers investigate cases consistently, assists taxpayers in providing accurate information and allows RSL to learn from completed cases over time.

The central principle is straightforward:

> **RSL should be able to understand the economic reality behind a taxpayer's declared position quickly, consistently and with clear evidence.**

Technology and artificial intelligence are used primarily to remove repetitive work, analyse large volumes of information and assist people with complex tasks. RSL officers remain responsible for all legal, tax, audit and enforcement decisions.

# 1. The Problem We Are Solving

Challenge 2 is not simply a problem of detecting suspicious tax returns.

The underlying challenge is that the true economic story of a taxpayer may be spread across many different sources: VAT returns, corporate income tax returns, invoices, customs declarations, financial statements, payroll, withholding tax records, contracts, payments, group structures, foreign counterparties and historical behaviour.

A suspicious result can therefore arise for several very different reasons.

It may represent genuine tax avoidance or evasion. It may result from missing or poor-quality information. It may be caused by a legitimate commercial arrangement. It may result from an incorrect taxpayer submission. It may expose a gap in current legislation rather than wrongdoing by the taxpayer.

The system must be capable of distinguishing between these possibilities.

The current Lesotho environment also creates several specific considerations. Lekuka and the Invoice Data Management System provide RSL with a stronger domestic invoice foundation, but foreign-issued invoices remain outside that system and historical digital information remains limited.

The August 2026 AfriSam judgment also demonstrates why risk analysis must remain tied to the law. A transaction appearing inconsistent with an arm's-length outcome does not automatically establish an adjustment under existing Lesotho legislation. Where the available law does not provide an appropriate route, the system should identify a potential policy gap rather than manufacture an enforcement conclusion.

The problem is therefore not simply:

**“Which taxpayers look suspicious?”**

The more useful question is:

**“What actually happened economically, what evidence supports that conclusion, what remains unexplained, and what lawful action is available to RSL?”**

# 2. Proposed Solution

Vati AI proposes a national tax-intelligence and investigation platform built around five connected capabilities.

| Capability | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Prevent and improve compliance** | Help taxpayers provide complete and accurate information before unnecessary problems arise. |
| **Detect and prioritise** | Identify cases requiring attention using transparent rules, behavioural analysis, relationships and emerging-pattern detection. |
| **Assemble and investigate** | Bring evidence together, reconcile information and carry out repetitive investigative work automatically. |
| **Resolve and govern** | Present evidence and legal routes clearly while keeping decisions with authorised RSL officers. |
| **Learn and improve** | Use reviewed outcomes, officer feedback, taxpayer behaviour and process results to improve future work. |

The result is not simply an analytical tool. It is a coordinated operating environment through which RSL can move from a risk signal to a complete, evidence-supported outcome.

---

# 3. Understanding Economic Reality

A central capability of the platform is the ability to reconstruct the economic reality of a taxpayer.

Rather than relying solely on a fixed list of known tax-evasion schemes, the system evaluates whether the different parts of the taxpayer's economic story are consistent with one another.

For every relevant transaction or arrangement, the platform considers questions such as who actually conducted the activity, who owns or controls the entities involved, whether the transaction actually occurred, what it was genuinely worth, how it should be classified, where and when it occurred, how the parties are related, whether relevant information has been omitted and whether the supporting evidence can be trusted.

This approach allows RSL to detect both known tax-risk patterns and new arrangements that may not yet match an established risk rule.

For example, the system may never previously have encountered a particular combination of companies, digital payments and invoices. It may nevertheless detect that the payments, invoices, declared revenue, ownership structure and trading activity cannot all be reconciled.

The platform therefore investigates inconsistencies in economic reality rather than depending entirely on predefined fraud labels.

---

# 4. Known Risks and Emerging Risks

The intelligence layer should operate through two complementary approaches.

**Known-risk intelligence** uses established RSL and international tax-risk indicators. These include persistent losses, related-party service charges, unexplained VAT credits, imported services without corresponding VAT or withholding treatment, unusual intercompany financing, expatriate arrangements and other known patterns.

**Emerging-risk intelligence** looks for behaviour that is unusual even when there is no existing rule describing the exact scheme.

This includes sudden changes in taxpayer behaviour, unusual transaction sequences, unexpected relationships between entities, repeated patterns across apparently unrelated taxpayers, unexplained contradictions between independent sources and clusters of companies exhibiting similar unusual behaviour.

The purpose is not to label unfamiliar activity as fraud.

It is to bring genuinely unusual activity to the attention of an RSL officer for review.

---

# 5. Entity, Ownership and Relationship Intelligence

Complex tax arrangements frequently involve more than one taxpayer.

The platform therefore builds an economic relationship view covering companies, individuals, directors, shareholders, beneficial owners, parent companies, sister entities, suppliers, customers, employees, consultants, bank accounts, invoices, payments and other relevant relationships.

This allows RSL to investigate the economic network surrounding a taxpayer instead of examining each declaration independently.

The system should also distinguish between legal ownership and beneficial ownership where reliable information is available.

Where ownership information conflicts between different sources, the conflict itself becomes visible to the investigator rather than being silently resolved by the system.

This capability also supports investigation of schemes involving several companies.

A suspicious VAT refund may initially appear to concern one taxpayer but later reveal common directors, common suppliers, common payment accounts or repeated invoices across several businesses.

The platform therefore allows several taxpayer cases to be connected to a broader **scheme investigation**.

---

# 6. Professional and Intermediary Relationships

Sophisticated tax arrangements can involve advisers and intermediaries.

The platform should therefore be able to identify where the same accountant, consultant, company-formation agent, customs intermediary or other professional repeatedly appears across separate cases.

This does not establish wrongdoing.

It provides RSL with relationship intelligence that may justify further review where an unusual pattern emerges.

This capability is particularly important because the system's objective is to understand networks of economic activity rather than make assumptions about isolated transactions.

---

# 7. VAT Refund Assurance

The proposed VAT refund process retains the three-lane model already developed for the solution.

| Lane | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Fast lane** | Refund is fully explained and reconciled. The system prepares a clearance package for officer approval. |
| **Standard lane** | Most of the claim is explained but specific information remains outstanding. Only the unresolved portion is targeted. |
| **Targeted investigation lane** | Material unexplained amounts, related-party issues, supplier risks or other significant indicators require specialist investigation. |

This approach protects revenue while improving service.

A large VAT credit should not automatically be considered suspicious. Structural credits caused by zero-rated outputs and standard-rated inputs should first be tested and, where supported, cleared efficiently.

The system should therefore be measured not only by how much additional revenue it identifies, but also by how effectively it reduces unnecessary investigation of legitimate claims.

---

# 8. A Stronger Taxpayer-Side Service

Poor information reaching RSL creates unnecessary work for both the taxpayer and the administration.

The proposed platform therefore includes a taxpayer-facing compliance and evidence service.

Instead of simply allowing a taxpayer to upload documents, the system assists the taxpayer in preparing a complete submission.

For example, before a VAT refund claim is submitted, the system may identify that most invoices reconcile successfully but that three invoices cannot currently be matched to imports and two are missing required supplier information.

The taxpayer is then given an opportunity to correct or explain those issues before RSL must manually investigate them.

Where an investigation has already begun, RSL can issue a targeted evidence request identifying the exact transactions or information requiring clarification.

The taxpayer can submit an explanation and supporting evidence directly against the relevant issue.

This creates a common structured evidence record for both RSL and the taxpayer and reduces unnecessary correspondence, irrelevant documents and repeated requests.

Over time, the system can also recognise recurring mistakes.

If a taxpayer repeatedly omits a particular type of supporting information, the platform can warn the taxpayer during future submissions.

The result is preventative compliance rather than relying exclusively on enforcement after an error has already occurred.

---

# 9. Investigation Playbooks

Different tax risks require different investigation methods.

The platform therefore uses approved investigation playbooks developed with RSL specialists.

A related-party service case, for example, may require examination of the relationship between entities, contractual obligations, evidence that services were actually provided, the commercial benefit received, possible duplication of local functions, payment evidence, tax treatment and the effect on local profitability.

An imported-services case may require financial-statement analysis, foreign payment information, withholding declarations, VAT treatment, contracts and foreign evidence.

The playbook tells the system what information to obtain and what questions to test.

It does not determine the legal conclusion.

This allows expert RSL investigation practice to become repeatable and consistent while preserving professional judgement.

---

# 10. Learning How RSL Officers Work

The system should also become better at supporting officers over time.

Where experienced investigators repeatedly perform the same sequence of legitimate tasks for similar cases, the platform can identify the repeated pattern and propose a reusable workflow.

For example, an officer may repeatedly review related-party disclosures, compare three years of financial statements, isolate management fees, examine contracts, check imported-service VAT and request proof that services were actually delivered.

After observing this repeated process, the system can propose a reusable investigation workflow.

The workflow does not become operational automatically.

It must first be reviewed and approved by the appropriate officer or supervisor.

Once approved, the routine steps can be performed automatically in future cases, leaving the officer to concentrate on interpretation and judgement.

The learning cycle is therefore:

**observe work → identify a repeatable process → propose a workflow → obtain approval → automate routine steps → measure results → improve.**

This allows RSL to capture specialist knowledge while avoiding the risk of turning an individual officer's habits into institutional policy without review.

---

# 11. Reusable Investigation Skills

The platform should maintain approved reusable capabilities that can be combined into different investigation workflows.

Examples include retrieving tax returns, matching invoices to customs declarations, identifying related parties, comparing financial periods, reconstructing transaction timelines, analysing profitability, identifying missing evidence, preparing targeted taxpayer requests and preparing a case briefing.

These capabilities allow new investigation playbooks to be created more quickly as RSL encounters new risks.

---

# 12. Evidence Confidence and Provenance

Not all information carries the same level of reliability.

The system should therefore maintain an evidence-confidence model.

Each important piece of evidence should record its source, date, taxpayer or entity connection, verification status, completeness, contradictions, relevant tax issue and whether it has been reviewed by an officer.

This enables the platform to distinguish between:

**missing information, unverified information, contradictory evidence and confirmed discrepancies.**

That distinction is essential.

A missing customs record should not automatically be interpreted as proof of a false VAT claim.

The system must first determine whether the issue may result from incomplete data.

Every significant conclusion should remain traceable to the underlying evidence. The existing solution already adopts evidence traceability and human-controlled decision-making as fundamental governance principles.

---

# 13. Testing Alternative Explanations

The system should not only search for evidence supporting a tax-risk hypothesis.

It should actively test reasonable legitimate explanations.

If persistent losses and significant related-party service fees are identified, for example, the system should also consider whether the losses may reflect market entry, expansion costs, genuine commercial difficulties, exchange-rate movements or documented services that produced genuine economic benefit.

This creates an internal challenge mechanism.

The objective is not for the system to prove its first suspicion.

The objective is to help RSL establish the most defensible explanation of the evidence.

---

# 14. Cross-Border Investigation and Exchange of Information

Challenge 2 inevitably requires foreign information because multinational arrangements extend beyond Lesotho's jurisdiction.

The platform should therefore support RSL's existing international information-exchange processes rather than attempting to create a separate international network.

Where an investigation identifies a material evidence gap outside Lesotho, the system can structure the information requirement, identify the foreign entity and jurisdiction, assemble the domestic evidence already available and prepare an exchange-of-information package for review by the appropriate RSL competent authority.

The request is only transmitted through existing lawful channels and after authorised human review.

When information returns from the foreign jurisdiction, it is linked directly back to the original investigation and compared with the taxpayer's domestic evidence.

This provides continuity between the domestic investigation and international cooperation.

---

# 15. Digital Payments, Crypto-Assets and New Financial Channels

Tax administration must increasingly account for economic activity taking place through different payment channels.

The platform should therefore treat payment information as a broad category rather than designing a separate system specifically for Bitcoin or another cryptocurrency.

Where information is lawfully available, the economic relationship model should be capable of incorporating bank payments, mobile-money transactions, foreign payments, crypto exchanges, blockchain wallet information and other relevant payment mechanisms.

Using a digital asset is not itself evidence of tax evasion.

The question remains whether the underlying economic activity has been appropriately declared and whether the associated income, expense or tax obligation can be reconciled.

The architecture should also be capable of accepting future international crypto-reporting information where Lesotho participates in such arrangements.

---

# 16. Tax and Customs Intelligence

For businesses involved in significant imports, tax and customs evidence should be considered together where legally and operationally appropriate.

An imported transaction may potentially produce several values: the customs value, supplier invoice value, accounting value, transfer-pricing value and actual payment value.

Differences may be entirely legitimate, but they should be capable of being reconciled.

Connecting relevant customs information with tax investigation can therefore improve VAT verification, related-party import analysis and broader economic understanding.

---

# 17. Serious Tax Crime and Inter-Agency Escalation

Some cases may move beyond normal tax administration into possible deliberate fraud or other financial crime.

The platform should therefore support a controlled escalation pathway.

A case begins as a tax-compliance or audit matter.

If evidence later suggests potential deliberate criminal behaviour, the case can be referred for specialist review.

Only authorised officials decide whether the legal threshold for further referral has been met.

Where appropriate and permitted by law, a controlled referral may then be made to the relevant RSL investigation function or other authorised national institutions.

The system records the reason for escalation, evidence transferred, authorisation and chain of custody.

Artificial intelligence cannot independently make criminal referrals.

---

# 18. Internal Integrity and Control

A modern tax-integrity system must also protect the administration itself.

The platform should maintain a complete audit trail showing who accessed a case, who changed information, who approved actions and where important controls were overridden.

Repeated unusual overrides or segregation-of-duty violations can be brought to an authorised supervisor's attention.

Such patterns are review signals, not accusations against officials.

This capability helps protect both RSL and its officers while improving accountability.

---

# 19. Institutional Knowledge

One of RSL's most valuable assets is the expertise developed by experienced officers and specialists.

That knowledge should not disappear when an employee changes role or leaves the organisation.

Completed investigations should therefore contribute to a controlled institutional knowledge base recording the type of case, issues investigated, evidence considered, explanations accepted or rejected, specialist reasoning, applicable law and final outcome.

Future investigators can use this knowledge to understand how similar issues were previously approached.

It does not automatically create legal precedent.

It provides institutional memory.

---

# 20. Learning from Objections and Appeals

A tax decision should not disappear from the learning process once an assessment is issued.

Where a taxpayer objects, appeals or successfully challenges an assessment, that outcome provides important information about the quality of the original investigation.

The platform should therefore connect:

**investigation → decision → objection → appeal → final outcome → learning.**

If a particular investigation approach repeatedly produces findings that are later overturned, the system should flag the relevant workflow, evidence standard or interpretation for management review.

This allows RSL to improve the quality and defensibility of future cases.

---

# 21. Policy Intelligence

Not every harmful revenue pattern is illegal.

The platform must therefore distinguish between non-compliance and gaps in legislation.

The existing legal-route approach already allows a finding with no available legal route to be recorded as a policy gap rather than incorrectly treated as an audit finding.

The proposed system extends this by analysing repeated policy-gap cases collectively.

If many taxpayers are using the same legally permissible structure and the result creates significant revenue exposure, the Deputy Commissioner and relevant policy authorities can be shown the pattern.

This gives RSL evidence to support future legislative or policy discussions.

---

# 22. Continuous Improvement Without Autonomous Decision-Making

The platform should improve over time, but improvement must be controlled.

The system may learn that certain risk indicators produce large numbers of false positives, while combinations of other indicators are more useful.

It may identify that a previously important pattern has become normal business behaviour.

It may also identify emerging patterns that deserve new investigation guidance.

These changes should first be tested against historical cases, reviewed for false positives and validated by RSL specialists.

Consequential changes to risk models, investigation procedures or tax interpretation should require appropriate human approval before deployment.

The principle is:

> **The system may learn continuously, but it does not govern itself.**

RSL remains responsible for its policies, procedures and legal interpretations.

---

# 23. Management and Assurance

Management requires a different view of the system from individual auditors.

The management workspace should show how the tax administration itself is performing.

This includes case backlogs, investigation duration, refund turnaround times, high-value unresolved cases, repeated data-quality issues, workloads, international-information delays, false-positive rates, investigation yield, model effectiveness and recurring patterns across taxpayers.

Management should also be able to identify operational bottlenecks.

For example, if investigations are being delayed primarily because taxpayer evidence requests remain unanswered or international information takes too long to obtain, that should be visible.

The objective is not simply to monitor staff.

It is to improve the operation of the tax system.

---

# 24. User Experience

Although the platform underneath will be complex, the user experience should remain simple.

An officer opening a case should not be presented with every database field available to RSL.

The system should answer five practical questions:

| Question | What the officer sees |
|---|---|
| **What happened?** | Concise description of the tax event or pattern. |
| **Why is this case here?** | Specific risk indicators or unresolved discrepancies. |
| **What has already been checked?** | Completed reconciliations and evidence reviews. |
| **What remains unresolved?** | Exact transactions, amounts, relationships or documents requiring attention. |
| **What should happen next?** | Appropriate next actions available to the officer. |

The technology should reduce complexity for the user rather than expose it.

---

# 25. Human Responsibility

The system is designed around human authority.

Automated tools may retrieve information, compare records, identify patterns, organise evidence, draft correspondence and recommend further investigation.

They may not autonomously assess tax, deny a refund, accuse a taxpayer of fraud, issue enforcement action, make a criminal referral or change RSL policy.

Those responsibilities remain with authorised officers.

This is central to the credibility and trustworthiness of the proposal.

---

# 26. Proposed Challenge 2 Pilot

The Taxathon prototype and initial pilot should remain focused.

The broader architecture can support additional tax risks later, but the immediate implementation should concentrate on the areas identified in Challenge 2.

The prototype should demonstrate five representative scenarios: a legitimate but unusual VAT refund that is cleared quickly; a high-risk related-party case involving persistent losses and material related-party charges; an ambiguous employment-versus-consultancy case requiring specialist judgement; an imported-services case involving foreign charges without a clear VAT or withholding trail; and a case where the economic issue appears concerning but existing law provides no clear enforcement route and the matter is therefore recorded as a policy gap. These scenarios are consistent with the current brief.

A further network or digital-payment scenario can be introduced to demonstrate how the architecture can respond to emerging methods without expanding the entire pilot beyond manageable scope.

---

# 27. Measures of Success

Success should not be measured solely by additional tax assessed.

A strong tax administration must protect revenue while treating compliant taxpayers efficiently.

The pilot should therefore measure valid-refund processing time, investigation preparation time, proportion of evidence assembled automatically, false-positive rates, audit hit rate, yield per audit hour, time spent by officers on repetitive preparation, taxpayer response time, quality of evidence submitted, number of unresolved evidence gaps, international-information turnaround where applicable, and the proportion of findings with a clear legal route.

The current solution already identifies cycle time, manual-review reduction, case-assembly time, audit yield, false positives and legal-route coverage as important measures.

---

# 28. Implementation Approach

The system should be introduced gradually.

The first stage should use a bounded group of Special Sectors, risk and refund officers, a defined FMCG taxpayer population, approved data extracts and synthetic data where live information cannot yet be made available.

The second stage should connect additional RSL information sources and strengthen taxpayer interaction.

Subsequent stages can add more investigation playbooks, deeper international cooperation, network analysis, additional tax types and additional RSL business areas.

This phased approach allows RSL to evaluate measurable benefits before broader deployment.

---

# 29. Long-Term National Value

Although Challenge 2 provides the initial use case, the underlying capability can support a much broader national objective.

The long-term opportunity is to create an **adaptive tax-administration intelligence capability for Lesotho**.

New investigation playbooks can progressively support additional risks and sectors without rebuilding the platform.

The same foundation could eventually assist with withholding tax, domestic VAT fraud, PAYE, customs-related risks, treaty abuse, permanent-establishment questions, digital services, high-net-worth taxpayers and other areas selected by RSL.

The value lies not in creating another standalone system.

It lies in allowing RSL's existing systems, people, evidence and institutional knowledge to operate together more effectively.

---

# 30. Proposed Value to Revenue Services Lesotho

The proposed system is designed to provide four outcomes simultaneously.

**For RSL officers**, it reduces time spent collecting, cleaning and repeatedly checking information so specialists can concentrate on judgement and investigation.

**For taxpayers**, it provides clearer requirements, more targeted information requests, assistance in correcting errors and faster resolution of legitimate claims.

**For RSL management**, it provides greater visibility into risk, workloads, investigation performance, emerging schemes, policy gaps and operational bottlenecks.

**For Lesotho**, it strengthens revenue protection, institutional knowledge and the country's ability to respond to increasingly complex domestic and cross-border tax arrangements.

---

# Final Proposal

Vati AI proposes that Revenue Services Lesotho pilot an **Adaptive Tax Intelligence and Investigation System** beginning with Challenge 2's multinational, related-party and VAT refund risks.

The platform would not replace RSL's existing tax systems, professional expertise or legal authority.

It would connect them.

It would help RSL understand taxpayers and transactions in their full economic context, identify both known and emerging risks, assemble and reconcile evidence, coordinate domestic and international investigations, assist taxpayers in providing better information, automate repetitive investigative work and preserve institutional knowledge.

Most importantly, the platform would improve continuously from reviewed outcomes while maintaining clear limits on automation and keeping consequential decisions with RSL officers.

The intended result is a tax administration that can investigate complex cases faster, clear legitimate taxpayers sooner, adapt to changing methods of avoidance and evasion, preserve specialist knowledge and produce decisions that are more consistent, transparent and defensible.

**The objective is not to replace the people responsible for Lesotho's tax system. It is to give them better tools, better information and more time to apply their expertise in protecting the country's revenue.**
